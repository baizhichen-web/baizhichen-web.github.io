**Summary** — 从家里的小卖部写起，回望二十二年的观察、设计与成长，把自己当作第一个设计对象。

“I am about to do a difficult work, one that has no precedent and will have no imitator. I mean to lay bare the true face of a man before the world, and that man is myself,” Rousseau said as much in his autobiography 《忏悔录》(The Confessions). I could never be such a figure, and I do not have the courage to lay my own chest bare for everyone to see.

But when I received such a notice and prepared to start writing this piece, I had indeed written some autobiographies before. What I am about to write now is unlike any of the articles I have written about myself before, and I hope it will not be a safe, bloodless one that might as well not have been written at all. In short, I am gathering my courage to write my own story; let Zweig take possession of me, and enter that world of yesterday.

## I.

Perhaps I should begin with a small grocery shop. It was already there before I was born — no more than a dozen or so square meters, yet it carried the youth of my parents' generation and my childhood. Sometimes you cannot help marveling at the wonders of the world, that decades of time could be compressed into so small a space.

At the dinner table I once heard my mother tell the shop's whole history — “This little shop was pieced together by your father. One day he was off to West Street to owe Zhang San a few thousand for rice; the next, to run up tens of thousands in cigarette money...” I could clearly hear the hardship of those years behind her deliberately light words. It is hard for me to imagine how my simple, honest father brought himself to ask others for money. Perhaps it was my grandfather's work at a supply and marketing cooperative before the reform and opening up that gave my father the idea of running a grocery shop.

I grew up in just such a shop. I did not leave until I was eighteen, though every summer vacation I would go back and mind the shop. I grew up standing behind the counter — which family's children liked which snacks, what cigarettes the chess and card room next door bought, all of it kept in my mind. Running a shop like this, the first thing it taught me was how to observe. As it happened, what design taught me was also observation —

It was like a small field of its own, like a milk carton resting exactly in the square frame on top of a fence. Unconscious design surfaced in this little shop, and I unknowingly practiced observing these unconscious acts, these small and real traces.

It was just such a shop that recorded the growth of a child, of a family, of a street, of a community.

After that came four years of study. Before entering this major — or rather, before my junior year — I knew very little about design. I always felt I had no remarkable talent for design, and not out of modesty; in those years I truly did not see in myself any particularly outstanding gift for it. The only thing I could boast of, perhaps, is that I was recommended for admission to 北京师范大学未来设计学院 (School of Future Design, Beijing Normal University) without taking the entrance exam. Most of the rest was workload, hard grind, and a curiosity a little greater than others'. If I had to attach one label to those four undergraduate years, I suppose it would be football.

From joining the department team as a freshman to later making the university team, the failures on the pitch brought me a great deal. Off the field, I would reflect on my own inadequacy, on failing to contribute what I could to the team; on the field, catching my breath as we saluted the crowd, I would review the game frame by frame — which decisions had been decisive and right, which moments had slipped away through hesitation.

Life holds many stories of turning adversity around, and people always like to stand on the side that turned the tables and sing of miracles. But when you become the backdrop for the other side, it feels entirely different. Even so, the process of turning inward and examining myself after every defeat has genuinely shaped the stance I take in the face of adversity.

## II.

Evaluating yourself is never easy. Few people can explain themselves very clearly. To write this section I racked my brains, and so dug out the thought reports from my four university years, one every quarter — perhaps my most frequent, most regular self-reflection. Once I wrote:

“Model Making Methods” — 76. Poor hands-on skills and little patience; and in my later design practice I never put the knowledge of building and testing mock-up models to good use.

“Computer-Aided 3D Design” — 84. I grew arrogant after picking up a bit of modeling knowledge in advance, lacking respect for the course, and never properly trained myself into a sound modeling ability.

Much of it was nonsense. So here, I think, I can attempt a synthesis once more. This is someone with poor hands-on skills; someone with no patience; someone whose enthusiasm lasts three minutes; someone afraid of toil and difficulty; someone who loves making grand declarations; someone who fancies himself clever.

But there must be some good sides to match. This is someone who loves sports; someone who loves to create; someone who is sunny and cheerful; also someone with a good temper; someone who longs to become honest, kind, and morally upright; and someone who keeps himself from growing any more base and vile.

## III.

I am not very satisfied with such a person. These past twenty-two years still hold many, many regrets. At this juncture of planning for the life ahead, I think I might as well first indulge myself in imagining my next life — as a student of design, or perhaps even design it.

The scope in which we design is always wide, from the individual and isolated to the more systemic. And of course, in the end the gaze can be turned back upon oneself, upon one's own life. Perhaps this is the ultimate design problem we have to face.

Perhaps it was 《好运设计》(Designing Good Luck) by Shi Tiesheng, which I read a while ago, that spurred me on; or perhaps it was the “life-design prompt” posted a while ago by a self-media blogger that inspired me —

Anyway, this is life, after all. It is hard to design it like a single blueprint drawn to the end and then built step by step, and I do not seek to be born broad-shouldered and long-legged, handsome and brilliant, rolling in money — that is the model Shi Tiesheng rejected, for such perfection is hardly happiness. But could we design a life structure with a higher tolerance for error? That is the model AI gave me, and I think there is something in it worth taking.

To begin with, allow yourself to be idle now and then, allow yourself to do things poorly sometimes, allow yourself to do what you want to do first rather than what is more urgent.

Next, what I have designed is a set of principles:

First, keep observing and recording. Open that curious eye wider, take in more knowledge from the world, from the community, from books, and keep for yourself a continually updated log.

Second, keep reviewing and correcting. Open this log from time to time and, as if reviewing old code, find the clumsiness, the hesitation, and the misjudgments.

Third, allow yourself to keep recalibrating your goals, because no one can be certain that the goals you set for yourself at any given moment will suit the self you will become in the future.

## IV.

The word limit (there was no room for many design rules precise to data and itemized to the last detail) and personal reasons (I truly cannot claim to be able to question my own heart, to see the future clearly, or to set for myself goals I can complete) mean that this autobiography is far from finished. Perhaps only when I can talk about myself calmly with someone, or thump my chest and say I know myself, could it be called complete. But for now, let me just do well what lies before me, and leave the future open to possibility and uncertainty.
