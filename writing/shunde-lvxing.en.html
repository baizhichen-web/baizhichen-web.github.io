Summary — 带舍友用圆周旅迹走完顺德三天两夜，把一次旅行当产品拆解——发心、定位、不可能三角与三个错位。

"Read ten thousand books, walk ten thousand miles." The ancients set walking beside reading, meaning books alone were not enough — you had to go and see for yourself. What they feared was reading too much and walking too little. Four hundred years later, with Xiaohongshu, Bilibili and Douyin, "reading ten thousand books" has become "scrolling ten thousand notes." "Knowledge is the beginning of action; action is the completion of knowledge" — and yet we have fallen into a different bind. We know too much, and no longer know how to set out.

How many guides saved in Xiaohongshu, how many videos stored on Bilibili, passed around in Douyin group chats, the map dotted with everywhere we want to go. We don't know how to connect those places into one route.

I feel this is what a travel product should really solve — what it truly sells should not be hotels, flights or tickets, but scattered information gathered into a single path you can actually set out on.

So when I got ready to travel to Shunde with my roommate, I gave 圆周旅迹 a serious go.

I. What Kind of App Is 圆周旅迹?

First — the Original Intent

"A designer by training and a P-type by nature, who found no simple, usable trip-planning product on the market and decided to build one. They were bold; we plucked up our courage and followed. That is how 圆周旅迹 began — Little Circle, to be exact, a growing travel-planning app and a group of us learning how to do 圆周旅迹 well while exploring the world."
—— The 圆周旅迹 developers

Product people like to talk about original intent. Confucian product people have their own version of it — "Stop where you should stop, and you will be settled; be settled, and you will be calm." What they stress is that intent comes first. A product's temperament lies, to a great degree, in the intent it began with.

As someone interested in product design, I was moved by 圆周旅迹's founding intent. As they put it, the state before every departure is resfeber — excitement and fear rushing into the body at once. Excitement because you are going somewhere new, fear because you don't know what you will meet. I wanted to melt that excitement into the fear and make my trip run like clockwork.

But honestly, what moved me more was the product's UI design. And its rather clever marketing on Xiaohongshu.

Second — Positioning

Starting from the founding intent — "a complete plan for the trip, an easy path to follow, and the surprise of the unknown" — we discussed its positioning. A product's positioning can really be broken down into a series of positionings.

User positioning — "for whom"

Young travelers aged 18-35 who prefer independent travel and want to plan efficiently; the "lazy travelers" who don't want to spend hours on guides; the organizers in any group trip. These people gather information from social platforms, but they don't fully trust the guides those platforms serve up.

Scenario positioning — "where it gets used"

The full cycle of trip planning. Inspiration gathering (copying homework from Xiaohongshu and the like), itinerary building (AI-generated routes and group collaboration), the trip itself (map navigation and on-the-fly adjustment).

Value positioning — "what problem it solves"

Guides scattered everywhere and hand-sorting them is slow work; no idea how to chain sights into a sensible route; high communication cost and out-of-sync information when traveling in a group; scrolling ten thousand notes and still no itinerary you can set out with.

Competitive positioning — "why us"

A pure planning tool — no booking, no flight or hotel sales; paste a Xiaohongshu or WeChat official-account link and it parses it into an itinerary in one tap; AI sequences stops by geography, with reliable cross-city transfers; completely free, no ads, no in-app purchases — trust built through subtraction.

This positioning is narrow — so narrow that its commercial path is hard to see. But it is also deep. One-tap import, automatic map routing, group collaboration — on OTA (Online Travel Agency) platforms these are side utilities; here they are the core experience.

In an internet industry where everyone wants to do everything, this restraint is itself a positioning skill. "The Way is lost day by day; lose and lose again, until you reach non-action." Learning means adding, day by day; getting things done usually means subtracting. A product is the same. The mark of a truly mature product is not more and more features, but an ever-clearer sense of which things should be left to others. We like to say it — not deciding what you will do, but deciding what you will not do. But for travel, is that enough?

II. Third — Design

Positioning answers "what should be done"; the hard part is "can it actually be done." Resfeber is the feeling of the moment the ticket is bought, while a trip runs three days, seven days, sometimes longer. The emotion fades, and what is left is a pile of small decisions. What time do we leave tomorrow? A first or B first? Taxi or subway? None of these solves itself just because the idea was good. The question is how to build it.

And so I have come to doubt whether trip planning is a sound product proposition at all.

The Impossible Triangle

What draws me most to 圆周旅迹 is that it caught a real need — how to gather up that pile of trivial decisions before a trip. Getting that right is genuinely satisfying.

The trouble is, the moment a product tries to solve this, it almost inevitably faces three goals that pull against each other.

First, save users time.

Second, make the planning genuinely useful.

Third, keep the product alive.

But these three things cannot all be done best at once. Pick two, and the third gets discounted. This is the product's "impossible triangle."

If you want users to generate a route book in one minute, you have to lean on existing data. If you want that route book to truly fit each person, you have to invest in a lot of interaction and let users keep correcting their preferences. And if you also want the product to be free, its ability to monetize is necessarily limited.

Angle one — personalization vs monetization

What is most compelling about travel is the personalized experience, not the standardized check-in at the hot spots. It is also why we increasingly hate the interchangeable guides on Xiaohongshu, with soft ads mixed in.

The biggest selling point of AI trip planning is "smart" recommendations. The actual experience is usually disappointing. Whether it is Dongshan Island or Xiamen, an AI-generated itinerary is nothing but influencer sights plus popular restaurants. It draws on marketing-flavored UGC from Xiaohongshu or Mafengwo, and what it recommends is highly interchangeable.

Polanyi said, "We can know more than we can tell." Travel is exactly like that. What a person loves or hates about a city comes largely from tacit knowledge that cannot be datafied — the smell of an alley kitchen, a teahouse at forty-five percent of menu price, the cross-breeze under the arcades. These experiences are what travel is made of, yet they can hardly become input for an AI.

I can believe AI has excellent logical reasoning. I will never believe it has excellent perception. Even a multimodal model — it has eyes, a mouth and ears, but no nose and no sense of touch. It will never know how to feel and experience the beauty you stumble into by accident while traveling.

What users really need is a distinctive experience; what AI can offer is the average of the masses — the "what most people would choose" computed out of big data.

Behind this sits a deadlock. A travel product cannot serve only the niche, because the niche is too hard to monetize. Influencer check-in spots get recommended again and again precisely because they bring commercial return. Free campsites, authentic hole-in-the-wall diners — how do you monetize recommending those? Without monetization the platform cannot survive. Charge users? Most travelers are already used to free.

The more personalized the planning, the harder it is to profit; the more standardized the planning, the less good users find it.

Angle two — planning complexity vs technical limits

What tempted me most was the AI planning feature.

Faced with a messy map after importing the places, how were we supposed to plan? I saw its AI planning and thought, naively, that it would lay out a route with good reasons — by my preferences, maybe, or by how easy the transport was.

People who build AI products all hope that AI can pull "what should be done" and "what should be visited" out of the flood of context — calendars, interests, bookmarks — and summarize and "intelligently" analyze them. That is one direction for AI.

But the plan it produced was entirely outside my imagination. It gave me three or four places a day, neatly split into morning, noon and evening. It had no idea I can eat three stops in a single midday, that I can finish Qinghui Garden and then head straight for Jinbang Street.

A day might cover seven or eight places; it can only auto-plan four or five.

"The man of Zheng who bought his shoes" — he trusted the ruler and not his own feet. Later generations treated it as a joke. Looking at it now, is AI trip planning not another man of Zheng buying shoes? It trusts the map, it trusts distance, and it never once asked what I wanted.

This is not a missing feature but a technical limit. What AI does today is mainly long-haul planning from origin to destination, while the real high-frequency need happens inside the destination, from sight to sight. The transport mode, time and route behind that are mostly a matter of calling map information, which is not technically hard. The hard part is how platform interests are divided — between OTA platforms and mobility platforms, who is entitled to present that answer.

Ideally, an agent could handle payment-involving decisions for the user automatically — booking flights, lodging, tickets. But these costs vary wildly, and in an environment full of instability and ambiguity, how does the agent find the optimal answer for the user? How do you define the agent's decision boundary and keep it from overstepping? These are the core difficulties of travel AI products, and almost no product has solved them well.

At a deeper level, users themselves do not fully know their own preferences. An unstable input cannot produce a stable output. So progressive interaction between AI and user information matters enormously — travel preferences and intent need to be designed as something that can be generated, revised and rejected. But on the market today, I have not seen a single AI travel product put its design weight here.

Whoever you are, it is hard to decide before departure that "this is exactly how I will play." Trip planning is, in essence, a false proposition — no one can lock down the whole itinerary in advance.

Planning usually means planning "a part" in advance and leaving the rest to be worked out on the road. How big that part should be, how much white space to leave, how to respond to change — the current product has no good answer for any of it.

Real travel decisions also happen segment by segment. Walk or taxi from A to B; change the plan going from B to C; switch the route when the weather turns. These are not solved by a single AI generation. AI in a product needs a closed loop of plan, execute, adjust — but current technology and product design can hardly deliver "faster, better, cheaper" all at once.

The core contradiction here — human decisions are non-linear, incremental, shaped by the mood and surroundings of the day. A real trip is always revising the plan. The weather changes, the route changes; one meal too many, the plan changes; you happen on a small shop, the plan changes again. A fixed route book is linear, one-off, locked in advance. What is needed is a route book with room to maneuver, flexible — and that kind of always-responsive function is, I think, something AI is good at. It just has not been brought out yet.

Angle three — shameless profit

If travel is inherently full of change, then the most stable thing is the transaction.

Hotels have to be booked, flights bought, tickets paid for. These are all certain needs.

So OTAs naturally have a bigger advantage than any independent trip-planning tool. They hold the users, they hold the merchants, and they hold the transaction itself. AI planning is just one more entry point on the transaction chain.

圆周旅迹 chose a different road.

It gave up hotels, flights and ads, and put nearly all its effort into planning itself — guide import, map routing, group collaboration, free to use. That is a very restrained positioning.

Users keep reminding the team in App Store reviews — "no ads, no in-app purchases, keep it the way it is."

The thing is, every restraint has a price. 圆周旅迹 chose simplicity and it chose free; so it has to accept one more thing — planning depth can hardly reach "smart" in any real sense.

Perhaps this is not 圆周旅迹's problem. It is travel's problem.

III. Three Mismatches

There are a few other functional problems.

Guide Import — Always a Patchwork

It can import a guide link from Xiaohongshu, parse the places automatically and generate an itinerary. But its logic is "import the whole link" — you copy a link, and it turns the entire guide into one route.

The problem is that I do not make guides this way. I take Xiaohongshu recommendations with a grain of salt; I would never paste in a whole link and use it intact. A little saved from Xiaohongshu, a little watched on Bilibili, a little filled in from YouTube, plus one line from a local friend — "skip that place" — and only then does my route slowly come together. A real guide is more like a jigsaw puzzle than an essay. In practice I search place names one by one and combine them by how near or far they are.

But the app has no such function. It assumes your information comes as "one complete guide," when the real scenario is "reassembling fragmented information." That is not just a missing feature; it is a mismatch between the product's assumptions and what users actually do.

When the ancients traveled, they had no guides; they asked the way. "Where is there a tavern?" — "there, past the apricot village," the shepherd boy says, pointing. One question, one pointing finger, and the path exists. Today our "asking the way" has become scrolling a feed. But there is too much information, and a lot of balanced, sincere advice drowns in an even greater volume of soft ads. In the end you don't know whose word to take.

So the problem is not that 圆周旅迹 cannot import guides. It is that it assumes information is naturally complete, when reality is the opposite — travel information is almost always fragmented.

Transport Planning — Decisions Are Context, Not Range

It cannot plan different transport modes for the leg from one place to another. What it sets is a range — preferred transport within 1 km, something else beyond 1 km.

But the transport decision in travel is "how do I walk this stretch," not "how do I walk within 1 km." A to B might be on foot, B to C by taxi, C to D by subway — the logic of each leg is different. The product's granularity (a range) does not match the user's decision granularity (leg by leg).

"Weigh, and you know heaviness and lightness; measure, and you know length and shortness." A real transport decision is never a fixed rule. It is weighing again on every leg.

Machines think in rules; users think in context.

Sorting Places — Good Interaction Should Not Need a Manual

Sorting is a pain. You have to remove a place and add it back, and it lands in the last position. In trip planning, reordering places is a high-frequency action — "A first or B first." When the cost of reordering is higher than the cost of re-planning, users give up on sorting.

(I only learned, while writing this piece, that places can be long-pressed and dragged. This is an affordance problem — users cannot discover the action by intuition.)

Good design should not require users to read a manual before they can understand the product. The right action should be visible in itself.

If a high-frequency function has to be discovered by accident, then in experience terms it does not exist.

(Amap could be the reference here — three parallel bars to signal that something can be dragged.)

IV. Three Days and Two Nights in Shunde

On the evening of Wednesday, June 24, I got off the train at Guangzhou South, ate a farmhouse roast goose at a place on the border of Foshan and Guangzhou, and headed straight for Shunde.

If an AI could have discussed with me whether to go to Shunde first or Guangzhou first, it might have changed the hotel I booked. It would have changed a whole series of planning decisions after that.

The farmhouse restaurant came from a local's recommendation. The hotel I booked through Huazhu (thanks to a corporate Platinum status). And from all the food-hunting videos on Xiaohongshu, Bilibili and Douyin, I marked every kind of food I wanted to go and check out. Then I would look for a sight nearby to walk it off.

The plan changed almost constantly.

The hotel breakfast was far more generous than expected, so there was no room for snacks along the way. The raw fish portion was far bigger than expected, so the steamed chicken we had planned had to wait until evening. To walk it off, we took an impromptu taxi to Shunfengshan Park and rode around it, lap after lap. One more restaurant today, one more street tomorrow; the weather changes, the plan changes; one "or maybe not" from whoever is with you, and the plan changes again. Travel is never executing a plan. It is revising the plan, over and over.

So, over the whole trip, I did not benefit much from the planning functions the system gave me —

To find a way, I still opened Amap; to get a taxi, I still opened DiDi. What kept bringing me back to 圆周旅迹 was not its planning but its recording. At each sight I would add a photo on the spot, note the price, write one line of comment. After a meal I would quietly put the taste, the queue time, whether it was worth a return visit into the itinerary.

Later, looking back at the map, what had been separate places were joined by the track into one complete trip. What really remained was not an AI-generated route book, but the traces of revising, filling in and recording all along the way.

In Six Chapters of a Floating Life, Shen Fu's portrait of Yunniang feels so vivid precisely because of those small, fragmentary details; reading them later is what makes them feel true. Travel is perhaps the same. Planning answers "where am I going"; recording answers "how did I once get there."

V. What I Hope For

Leaving everything else aside, this app can be called a rather good recording tool.

The planning has the problems above, but using it to record a trip is unexpectedly good. The places you have been are tracked automatically. Switch to the map view and each day's route is drawn as a line — you see at a glance what you walked. A friend on the same trip kept asking me to send him the itinerary map, but all I could do was screenshot it; the app does let you copy a link and send it to a WeChat friend. But does sharing have to cross platform after platform? Is there a lighter way to share and present? Could it support more flexible long-image customization, or export to PDF or Markdown?

People do not buy a drill. They buy a hole.

By the same token, could it be that people plan a trip in order to share how well-ordered the trip was? It might become a very good travel recording tool. Planning is what it wants to do; recording is what users actually use. The gap between the two is itself a product insight.

So, given what the product is now — it is not a good travel-planning product, so could it play up its recording side instead?

How can planning happen in real time?

How can it discuss the user's plan with them, progressively and wherever they are?

How can users drop their own sharp takes on sights, food and places into the itinerary?

Could the exported images and file formats be easier to share and to keep?

Could there be a fuller, more generous UGC community for recommendations?

In my not-fully-formed idea, how should we solve the triangle? Light planning, heavy recording.

Using "recording + discovery" to solve the triangle at once

A travel-recommendation community carries product monetization

A lightweight product architecture (no flights, no hotels) plus a strong route-planning feature ensures planning quality and saves users time

Solving the Impossible Triangle

Back to the impossible triangle — saving time, monetization, planning quality. Three things that cannot all be done best at once.

But the problem may lie in the word "planning." Trip planning is in essence a false proposition — no one can decide in advance that "this is exactly how I will play." Planning can only cover "a part"; the rest has to be worked out on the move. If planning is inherently unstable, then the product has no need to fixate on delivering one permanently correct answer. Make planning light, and do a good job of being a usable travel recording app.

Light planning does not mean giving up planning; it means admitting that planning is, after all, only "a part" of the trip. Heavy recording does not mean starting to record after the trip ends; it means letting recording become the trip itself.

"To be born is to be dying; in dying, to be born." Travel is the same. Plans keep failing; records keep being made.

Perhaps a truly good travel product should not try to arrange the user's future, but help the user understand everything that has already happened.

VI. The End

"When the spring runs dry, the fish lie together on the land, blowing on each other to stay damp, moistening each other with spit — better to forget each other in the rivers and lakes."

Guides are the same. Stripped of the specific time, weather, companions and how your body feels, even the most finely made guide is no more than fish spit on the bank. A real trip is never copying someone else's path. It is completing your own path by departing from the plan again and again.

Planning always happens before the trip; recording happens during it. The former tries to predict the future; the latter faithfully preserves everything that has already happened. One faces "what has not happened yet," the other faces "what has already happened."

Travel is often a matter of doing what man can and leaving the rest to heaven. What truly moves you is usually what could not be planned in advance. The planning function is the "usefulness of the useful" it worked so hard on, but what really kept me was the recording function it never designed for — "the usefulness of the useless is the great usefulness."

A planning that arrived too late, plus the photos I took along the way and the comments I jotted down, made up my three days of travel.
