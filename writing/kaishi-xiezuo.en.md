## I. Asking the AI, and LMGTFY

I was arguing with a friend about some question, right in the thick of it, when he suddenly stopped. "Forget it — let me go ask AI in a bit."

Right around then I happened to read a blog post titled, of all things, "Stop Telling Me To Ask An LLM"【1】.

![Screenshot of the String Literal post “Stop Telling Me To Ask An LLM” (8 July 2026)](https://baizhichen-web.github.io/media/writing/kaishi-xiezuo/fig-01.webp)

The author opens with a story from his own life. He had a thorny question, no industry consensus, and even after working through a pile of research it stayed unsolved. So he made a point of meeting a veteran with thirty years of experience, someone who had lived through bad calls, hoping for the kind of judgment that only settles out of painful lessons — the kind a search engine cannot give you. The man's answer was — "Honestly? Ask Claude."

The author recalls how LMGTFY【2】 links used to be a jab at people who could not be bothered to run a basic search. Today's "just ask AI" feels more like being handed a food ranking while the other person refuses to tell you "here is what I personally think is off about it."

Exactly. When it comes to food rankings, I am a decent local guide — whenever friends come to visit my hometown I steer them to the local eats I grew up on. When people ask for your opinion, what they are really after is your experience. What everyone cares about is finding the most distinctive food, or at least food that stands for what I hope they will taste.

From a user-research angle you could put it this way — every person in the world stands for one user group, one attitude. The answer they give you is the voice of that attitude, an attitude that exists outside AI and can help us make decisions. In a sense that is good user research — or rather, the author thought it through carefully before going to this particular person. After all, this person has his own embodied experience.

"Where there are mechanical contraptions, there will be mechanical deeds; where there are mechanical deeds, there will be mechanical minds."【3】

I could not help but glance at the feed apps on my phone. A piece of information gets produced, received, and consumed a second time over — and not one human judgment may have taken part the whole way. My Feishu (Lark) bot pings me about new content from yesterday's AI knowledge-sharing group; a summarizer condenses it into four bullet points; my writing bot suggests I quote it and has already drafted the paragraph for me. All I have to do is nod. The interface even thanks me.

It sounds like the setup of some dystopian science fiction, but it could just be an ordinary Monday.

"The five colors blind the eye; the five tones deafen the ear."【4】

Wu Zixu once cited this saying to talk the King of Wu out of accepting the beauties offered by the state of Yue【5】. I think I need it too, as a reminder to myself of how to treat the information AI keeps presenting to me.

## II. What Future Are We Running Toward

Looking back, the Industrial Revolution outsourced our limbs and torsos. Today's information age has brazenly begun "cutting down the fiefs" — this time, of the brain.

With every wave, one human ability gets stamped as inefficient and handed over to the machines. The steam engine did more work than horses; the computer stores more data than the brain; and an AI tool will always know your next typed word before you do.【6】

fast.ai founder Jeremy Howard recently sat down with Chris Lattner for a conversation. Jeremy says what he sees is developers abandoning every practice that leads to understanding, mastery, and durable software. CEOs boast about teams shipping ten thousand lines of AI-generated code a day; engineers proudly call what they do "vibe-coding." And Vibe Coding has spread across society at large — the old and the young are all using it, nobody bothering to understand it deeply, just feeling out AI's temper and getting by.【7】

![Cover of “Build to Last” — the Jeremy Howard × Chris Lattner conversation](https://baizhichen-web.github.io/media/writing/kaishi-xiezuo/fig-03.webp)
*Cover of “Build to Last” — the Jeremy Howard × Chris Lattner conversation*

What kind of future are we running toward?

When you tread on frost you can feel the hard ice coming【8】**,** and though the future holds all manner of strange and bizarre scenes we can imagine, one thing I believe is certain — diversity.

Look back, and species evolution runs on endless mutation and recombination. Genetic diversity is life's stake against total annihilation.

Look forward, and because I love science fiction I dream up all sorts of problems that have already been foreseen. Asimov's Foundation (《基地》) builds a statistics-based "psychohistory" on the premise that human behavior is predictable in aggregate — but the Mule breaks every prediction. The Mule is a mutant, an outlier beyond the statistical model. The Seldon Plan can predict the rise and fall of an entire galactic empire, but it cannot predict one person who refuses to play by the rules. Diversity is the natural enemy of prediction and control.【9】

The same goes for education — of course we want to see more diversity in our children. They are our future.

Sorting every child into a class by level — the gifted class, the rocket class, the innovation class, the remedial class — can only maim each child's individuality and appetite for exploration, cutting the feet to fit the shoes. Ni Minjing, director of the Shanghai Science and Technology Museum, told a story in his Yixi Youth talk. In an eighth-grade physics class on the conservation of energy, the smartest kid gets it at a glance. Then the "dumbest" kid raises his hand and asks — if energy works like that, why is there an energy crisis? And the smartest kid is asked to answer. Teacher, I have no idea either.【10】

![Ni Minjing on stage at 一席少年 — tracking by level only misfits every child](https://baizhichen-web.github.io/media/writing/kaishi-xiezuo/fig-04.webp)
*Ni Minjing on stage at 一席少年 — tracking by level only misfits every child*

Confucius had three thousand disciples, seventy-two of them virtuous. Zilu was brave, Zigong eloquent, Yan Hui content in poverty, Ran Qiu multitalented. The Master taught each according to his nature, never pressing them into a single mold【11】. Education is not about stamping out many identical geniuses; it is about creating and nourishing soil where innovation can emerge on its own. Innovation is not afraid of foolishness — it is afraid of sameness. The development of AI is no different.

In The Three-Body Problem (《三体》), the sophons lock down fundamental physics. Humanity can still maneuver in applied technology, but it can never break through that ceiling again.

Is it possible that, through AI, we are locking down our own creativity in a different way? —

My worry is that by the time scaling law runs out, the old data is used up, and the new data grows homogeneous, innovation will have become water without a source.

This worry has an exact counterpart in technology — it is called "model collapse (Model Collapse)【12】." When synthetic data generated by AI repeatedly becomes the material for the next round of training, the long-tail expressions — the rare, the off-average — die first, the data distribution narrows with each generation, and it finally collapses into a monotonous repetition devoid of information. Ted Chiang wrote something similar in Exhalation (《呼吸》) — a universe that runs on pressure differences, where everything stops once all the pressures even out.【13】

AI training chases the mean, and AI's output flows back into the training data. Each generation is more AI, more homogeneous than the last — and we are already living inside a system built of AI. With every breath we take in and out air laced with AI-generated content. We are accelerating this process of equilibration.

But here a sharp reader would cut me off. The real breakthrough lies in this — once we hit that "singularity," once AI masters the method of innovation, it could keep upgrading itself through self-training and finally arrive at AGI. In Liang Wenfeng's words, that means first the ability to keep learning like a human, and then self-iteration【14】.

From Zhang Xiaojun's interview with Zhang Peng【15】I learned that intelligence studies is about creating new knowledge on the foundation of existing information. Its knowledge production takes old knowledge into deductive reasoning and extracts new knowledge from it. If humans can do it, why not AI?

But intelligence studies does not work only that way. If humans could only deduce, we would still be stuck in the age of Aristotle. What truly pushes the boundaries of knowledge outward is something else — induction, and embodied cognition of the real world. Induction distills new laws from individual facts; it can be overturned by the next counterexample at any time, yet it is precisely what supplies knowledge not contained in the premises. Embodied cognition is a physicist's understanding of "force," a designer's intuition for "materials," a doctor's insight into "an illness" — all of it drawn from real sensory experience and feedback from the physical world. This kind of "friction" is what lets humans build an intuition for cause and effect.

These are exactly what coding-focused models do not sufficiently have —

At present, the moments when AI comes closest to "innovation" are when it plays move 37 like AlphaGo【16】, or when it independently discovers molecular structures in materials science that humans never thought of【17】. Closed worlds with clear rules. Go's wins and losses are utterly objective; the stability of a molecular structure can be computed exactly with an energy function. In these domains AI can indeed rely on brute-force computation and reinforcement learning to discover blind spots that humans miss. Yet when we turn to more complex scientific frontiers — the nature of dark matter, the origin of consciousness — we do not even know what the search space looks like, and we cannot define what a "good" reward function would be. In these domains, AI's "physical-world feedback" still depends heavily on humans to set the problem frame and the evaluation criteria.

## III. Subtraction

In the end, our hope has to be staked back on the "human." Let us turn our gaze from the grand future and the wide world back onto ourselves — and talk about the concrete problems we meet in daily life.

First — you do not need AI to help you see more. What you need is to do subtraction.

I often see comments under videos @-ing all sorts of AIs to summarize them. The video creator, who has to worry about completion rate, probably has complicated feelings — "what you get from others always feels shallow; to truly know a thing, you must do it yourself." And that is before you consider — without reading it yourself, what you receive is secondhand knowledge from someone else's video, which you then turn into thirdhand knowledge through an AI summary.

![Comment sections now full of @-ed AIs asked to summarize (screenshot)](https://baizhichen-web.github.io/media/writing/kaishi-xiezuo/fig-05.webp)
*Comment sections now full of @-ed AIs asked to summarize (screenshot)*

Information passes through the brain like clouds crossing the eye, gone by the next day. This seemingly efficient shortcut is in fact the greatest waste of life. Better to settle down and genuinely read it once, do it once.

"Read a book a hundred times, and its meaning will appear of itself." Memory and understanding themselves need the settling of time and the hard searching of the brain.

And what does an AI summary count as?

Traditional "knowing" has two carriers. From the perspective of Michael Polanyi's theory of tacit knowledge, they divide as follows【18】 —

Explicit knowledge — knowledge that can be fully expressed through symbol systems such as language, writing, formulas, and charts. It exists objectively and spreads and shares easily. Think of the theories in books, operating manuals, database code.

- Tacit knowledge — knowledge that is hard to articulate clearly, deeply rooted in personal experience, intuition, and mental models. Think of the sense of balance in riding a bicycle, the practiced feel of an old Chinese-medicine doctor taking a pulse, a designer's aesthetic intuition.

What AI produces ought to be called explicit knowledge. But given AI's own limitations, the hallucinations it may produce, and the biases baked in by the development team and the training data, AI summaries seem as if they ought to be perfectly accurate — and yet seem more likely to be wrong.

Even if an AI summary is 100% faithful to the original, you have still lost the process of conversing with the author. Reading is an active construction of meaning — you have to fill the gaps in the author's argument with your own understanding.

Silicon Valley investor Naval Ravikant writes in The Almanack of Naval Ravikant (《纳瓦尔宝典》) — "*I would rather read the best one hundred books over and over again, until I absorb them, than to read all the books.*" (I would rather read the best hundred books deeply and thoroughly than skim through every book there is.) Rereading yields something new every time because your own circumstances have changed【19】.

You have AI summarize something into three sentences. You got the information, but the lived experience of chewing it over again and again, of "reviewing the old and knowing the new" — a summary cannot give you that. The value of good content lies not in its volume of information but in its power to change the way you think about problems.

Our time has always been finite, and the information and content we see each day is finite too.

In this era, the real problem has never been how to acquire massive amounts of information, but how, in that ocean of information, to do subtraction, to filter finely, and to find the things worth chewing over again and again.

The Chinese AI influencer Zhang Zhala holds this view — he tells us to go to YouTube, go to Twitter, follow the key people, read first-hand information (builder, not influencer)【20】. My "do subtraction" argument comes from the blogger Zhang Siji, who also says his content comes from the long podcasts and tweets of the very best AI practitioners, people like Andrej Karpathy, plus classic books. His information sources are actually very few, but he reads every one of them carefully.【21】

## IV. Listening Widely, Seeing Clearly

"Listen broadly and be enlightened" runs in the opposite direction from "do subtraction" —

We have always wanted to break out of the information cocoon by taking in more information. With that in mind, I think the relationship between a person and AI can be framed as the relationship between an emperor and his ministers. Feudal rulers mostly worried about being hoodwinked by treacherous ministers, and an AI that does nothing but flatter is exactly like a sycophant official. We truly cannot listen narrowly or trust narrowly — whether the minister's role is played by AI or by the motley crew of our information sources.

On the one hand, you need to cut information away — do subtraction, read a small number of things closely and carefully.

On the other hand, you need to open wide the channels of speech — break out of the information cocoon by taking in more information, and synthesize an objective view.

You must "do subtraction" to guard against information overload, and you must "listen broadly" to guard against being trapped in one corner. Having both at once seems like a paradox.

The two are not in conflict. They simply apply to different stages.

My approach is this —

Subtraction targets your stage of "internalization." Once you have settled on what deserves deep reading, gnaw on the best content over and over, the way Naval does — actively carrying out the process of constructing meaning.

Listening broadly targets your stage of "screening." Here you are not after depth but after breadth and difference — use AI, use your established matrix of sources, to scan quickly.

So AI can perfectly well play the role of the remonstrating minister who "listens broadly" — distilling the outlines of podcasts, long essays, and reports for you. But you must always keep a string taut in your mind — this is only drawing up the shortlist, not the end of the story. Once you have circled the range of content, you have to shut AI outside the door. Facing the half-finished material you processed together with AI, use your own brain to connect, to combine, to search hard, and to practice.

"Study broadly, inquire carefully, think prudently, discern clearly, and practice earnestly."【23】

We refuse to outsource these five steps to AI's one-click summaries and inductions.

## V. Learning by Expressing

> If you want to master something, teach it. The more you teach, the better you learn.【24】
> —— The Feynman technique (Richard Feynman; see note 24)

So said Richard Feynman, and the Feynman learning technique is known to all.

The same idea comes from zara and Zhang Siji — both believe you need to build a mechanism that lets you keep producing output, or, to put it another way, to learn by expressing.【25】

![From the PROLIFIC BUILDING conversation — build a mechanism that keeps you creating](https://baizhichen-web.github.io/media/writing/kaishi-xiezuo/fig-07.webp)
*From the PROLIFIC BUILDING conversation — build a mechanism that keeps you creating*

AI is exploding right now. I believe everyone's starting point will not differ by much, but there is definitely a gap between thinking and application. So I think I need to record what I want to say and the process of my learning.

As for how to actually do it, I have tried many methods along the way. Especially when the LLM Wiki【26】concept was trending a while back, I kept trying to build an environment friendly to both myself and AI, and to construct a private knowledge base inside it. But once you hand AI too much power, it starts forcing far-fetched connections — the notes are not ones I took, the links are not ones I drew, and behind that vast-looking atlas of knowledge I had produced nothing at all. The push and pull of building a human-AI collaborative space could fill several more thousand-word essays.

My practice nowadays is this — use AI for the preliminary sorting and linking of material, stringing together the notes, articles, and ideas scattered across my bookmarks and knowledge base, tying the fragments of information floating between me and AI into a line through writing. Like Scheherazade, who married the king of her own will to save innocent women and, one story a night, wove together the Arabian Nights (《一千零一夜》)【27】 — I too string together the information and material I have gathered, and produce an essay like this one.

I choose to publish these essays on platforms like Xiaohongshu (RED) or WeChat official accounts — on social media, in other words. It feels to me like Forrest Gump (《阿甘正传》)【28】 — Gump rattling off the story of his whole life without a care for who was around him, on that bench, while the people beside him kept changing. Some stopped to listen, some walked away, and now and then someone would nod or ask a question.

If you have read this far, thank you very much! Whether it is me or Gump, I think any of this gives me a little feedback. Whatever the outside world makes of it, the act of expression itself gives me a kind of force to push back against the void.

Of course, sometimes what I tell you will be a good story as sweet as chocolate, and sometimes a bad story as harsh as dog crap. There are good views and bad views, correct views and wrong views — but whether good or bad, right or wrong, every one of them is an outpouring of my own embodied experience.

In short, I ask for your indulgence.

## VI. Dwelling on Things, and Meaning in Things

Su Shi also invoked the line in his "Record of the Hall of Treasured Paintings" (《宝绘堂记》)【29】 —

"The five colors blind the eye, the five tones deafen the ear, the five flavors dull the palate."

While warning his friend Wang Shen "not to fix your attention on things," he also offered an exquisitely subtle solution — "you may lodge your meaning in things."

So I say our attitude toward AI should be the same — mind the balance between "fixing attention" and "lodging meaning" —

To "fix attention on things" is to be driven by things, to sink into slavery to algorithms and information and lose your subjective agency. To "lodge meaning in things" is to take all things as instruments for entrusting your feelings and tempering your intellect — to play with things without losing your purpose.

**I have muddled through all of that, so to sum it up in one sentence — the ultimate value of the human lies in the diversity of embodied experience, while what AI gives us is the "mean" of the probability distribution most likely to fit the context above. We need to pay more attention to human value and human feeling — the friction between people and the world is what sparks creativity. So, how to collaborate with AI — writing is my answer.**

## Notes on the Citations

What follows are explanations of the various 【citations】 in the text —

**1. "Stop Telling Me To Ask An LLM"**

In her blog post "Stop Telling Me To Ask An LLM," Yael Grauer points out that when someone has already tried a large language model (LLM) themselves and still has questions, being told to "go ask the model" is actually a brush-off. She argues that this response seems polite but is really a euphemism for "I don't know" or "I don't have time." The key point is that a model cannot offer views and insights grounded in a person's unique life experience — and that is precisely the fundamental reason people consult a particular person in the first place. So Yael suggests that instead of dodging with "go ask the model," it is more honest to answer with "I'm busy" or "I can't think of anything you haven't tried yet."

**2. LMGTFY (Let me Google that for you)**

LMGTFY, short for "Let Me Google That For You," literally means "let me Google that for you." It comes from a satirical website of the same name. You type in a question, and the site auto-generates an animated link that demonstrates, step by step, typing the keywords into the Google search box. Send that link to the person who asked, and they will see the process of "getting the answer with a simple search" play out. LMGTFY thus became a humorous but sharp reminder of the early internet — a hint that you should try searching for yourself before asking. Today it is often used as a cultural symbol for reflecting on netiquette and information literacy.

**3. "Where there are mechanical contraptions, there will be mechanical deeds; where there are mechanical deeds, there will be mechanical minds"**

From the Zhuangzi (《庄子·外篇·天地》, "Heaven and Earth" in the Outer Chapters). The line comes from the story of the old gardener who carried water in a jar and refused to use a well-sweep (a lever for drawing water). It speaks directly to the erosion of the human heart by tools and technology. "Mechanical contraptions" means devices that save effort through clever design; "mechanical deeds" are the calculating ways of acting that follow from them; and a "mechanical mind" is the scheming, game-playing, profit-seeking impulse that sprouts inside. Through the old farmer's mouth Zhuangzi warns that once you depend on the efficiency shortcuts tools provide, your actions drift toward opportunism, and over time your inner simplicity is replaced by the calculus of power and advantage — "once the mechanical mind is lodged in the breast, pure whiteness is no longer complete," and the clarity and fullness of the spirit fall apart under utilitarian calculation. Across a thousand years, in an age of accelerating technological iteration, this line is often revived as an early warning of "technological alienation." It is not only a wariness of tools themselves but points to a habit of cognition — when "how to reach the goal faster" becomes the only measure, the primal questions of "why do it" and "what is true" quietly slip away. It still questions every individual caught in the flood tide of efficiency.

**4. "The five colors blind the eye; the five tones deafen the ear"**

From the "Nine Methods" chapter of the Yuejue Shu (《越绝书·内经九术》), it adapts the sense of the Daodejing's (《道德经》) "the five colors blind the eye, the five tones deafen the ear." It means that excessive sensory stimuli — colors, tones, and the like — will dull people's sight, hearing, and insight. The line aims to warn people to guard against small beginnings and avoid having their inner minds obscured by the clutter of external things.

**5. Wu Zixu's use of the saying**

Recorded in the "Nine Methods" chapter of the Yuejue Shu (《越绝书·内经九术》). This is a classic recording the history, geography, and philosophy of the Wu and Yue states in the Spring and Autumn period (traditionally attributed to Yuan Kang and Wu Ping of the Eastern Han), and it is praised as "the ancestor of Chinese local gazetteers." "Nine Methods" is its core chapter, setting down the nine strategies with which the Yue minister Wen Zhong helped King Goujian strengthen Yue and destroy Wu. The chapter blends Laozi's Daoist thought with political wisdom, advocating "cultivate the mind within, follow the patterns without," and is a concentrated expression of classical strategy and self-cultivation.

**6. The three-stage evolution of outsourcing ability**

This idea adapts Joan Westenberg's long English essay on Medium (2025), "How Cognitive Offshoring is Killing Personal Thought." The essay lays out a three-stage evolution of "cognitive offshoring" — the Industrial Revolution outsourced physical labor, the information age outsourced memory, and the age of AI is now outsourcing thought itself. The essay warns that when algorithms pre-empt our expressions, ultimate efficiency strips away the "friction" that thinking requires, leaving humanity with a vast store of answers but bereft of spiritual weight.

**7. The Jeremy Howard and Chris Lattner conversation**

From the conversation article "Build to Last," published in October 2025 by the AI research platform fast.ai. fast.ai founder Jeremy Howard talks with Chris Lattner, creator of LLVM and the Swift language, and delivers a sharp warning about the "vibe coding" culture popular in the generative-AI era. The article points out that over-relying on AI to deliver code while abandoning any understanding of the underlying architecture will lead to a cliff-edge decline in engineering ability. The real technical moat still lies in holding to "first principles" and pursuing the software craftsmanship of excellence.

**8. "Tread on frost and you can feel the hard ice coming"**

From the first-six line text of the Kun hexagram in the Book of Changes (《周易·坤卦》) — "tread on frost, and solid ice is coming." Originally it means that when thin frost is under your feet, you should already sense that the hard ice of deep winter is about to arrive. The ancients used it to expound the philosophy of "gradual accumulation" and "nipping trouble in the bud" — great transformations and crises never form overnight but accumulate step by step from tiny signs. It warns people to keep a keen eye, so that when change first shows itself they can forestall it.

**9. Psychohistory and the Mule in Foundation**

From the classic science-fiction series Foundation (《基地》) by the American grand master Isaac Asimov. In the books, the scholar Hari Seldon founds "psychohistory," whose core assumption is that when the population base is large enough, the macro-historical behavior of humanity can be predicted precisely with mathematics and statistics — the "Seldon Plan." Yet the appearance of the mutant "the Mule" breaks the model. As an individual with the psychic power to control emotions, he is an extreme outlier lying entirely outside statistical probability, and he overturns the macro-forecast single-handedly. In modern discussions, "psychohistory" stands for a faith in prediction built on big data, probabilistic algorithms, and determinism, while "the Mule" symbolizes the unpredictable aberration, the black-swan event.

**10. Ni Minjing's Yixi Youth talk**

"Exploring the Cave Full of the Unknown" (《探索充满未知的洞》), a talk given by Ni Minjing — director of the Shanghai Science and Technology Museum and former deputy director of the Shanghai Municipal Education Commission — on Yixi Youth (#Society / Wuxi / 2026.04.25).

**11. Confucius teaching according to ability**

"Three thousand disciples, seventy-two of them proficient in the six arts" — from the "Hereditary House of Confucius" and "Biographies of the Disciples of Zhongni" (《仲尼弟子列传》) in the Records of the Grand Historian (《史记》). In the Song dynasty, Zhu Xi summed up Confucius's educational philosophy in his Collected Commentaries on the Analects (《论语集注》) as "Confucius taught people, each according to his material." Facing disciples of differing character and talent, Confucius taught each in accordance with his nature — like Zilu, who was brave and resolute (the Shiji records him as "fond of bravery and strength"); Zigong, eloquent and quick of speech (the Analects places him first among the "speech" category); Yan Hui, who lived on a single basket of rice in a mean lane, content in poverty and devoted to the Way (the Analects praise him, "Virtuous indeed is Hui!"); and Ran Qiu, variously skilled and good at administration (the Analects say "Qiu, too, has abilities").

**12. Model collapse (Model Collapse)**

A degenerate phenomenon proposed in AI academia — represented by the landmark 2024 paper in Nature, "AI models collapse when trained on recursively generated data." It refers to the irreversible degradation that occurs when generative AI systems (such as large language models and diffusion models) are trained recursively for a long time on "synthetic data" generated by previous generations of AI. In this process, the long tail of the data distribution — the rare, the off-average, the marginal unique expressions and outliers of humanity — dies first. As generations advance, the distribution of the data the models generate narrows with each pass, variance plummets, and it finally collapses into a homogeneous state devoid of information, monotonously repetitive, and detached from the real world. This phenomenon proves, at the level of mathematics and probability, that genuine human data and embodied experience are irreplaceable.

**13. Ted Chiang's "Exhalation"**

From the short story "Exhalation" (《呼吸》) by the contemporary Chinese-American science-fiction writer Ted Chiang (collected in the 2019 collection of the same name). The story constructs a heterogeneous universe of metallic mechanical life. These beings' thought depends entirely on the "pressure difference" between the high-pressure argon in their inflated lungs and the outside atmosphere, which drives tiny cylinders and gears in their brains. As they breathe, the high-pressure gas keeps spewing into the atmosphere, so the overall pressure of the universe gradually rises and the difference between inside and outside keeps shrinking. Once the pressures approach absolute equilibrium, gas stops flowing, the mechanical brains can no longer compute or think, and all life and civilization in the universe falls into deathly silence. The work is a literary parable of the second law of thermodynamics (the law of entropy) and of information theory, revealing a physical and philosophical truth — "thought and life depend on gradients, differences, and friction; absolute equilibrium and homogeneity mean death." The essay uses it to figure the ultimate state of model collapse — the entropy of information reaching its maximum, with nothing new left.

**22. "Listen broadly and be enlightened; trust narrowly and be benighted"**

From the Zhenguan Zhengyao (《贞观政要·卷一·君道》, "Discourse on the Way of the Sovereign," Book I), written by Wu Jing of the Tang dynasty (also found in the Zizhi Tongjian, Tang Annals VIII (《资治通鉴·唐纪八》)). **23. "Study broadly, inquire carefully, think prudently, discern clearly, practice earnestly"**

From chapter 20 of the "Doctrine of the Mean" (《中庸》) in the Record of Rites (《礼记》), a Confucian classic. It names five progressive stages of classical Confucian learning and self-cultivation —

broadly ranging over and absorbing knowledge and information; raising questions and inquiring in detail and with care; thinking and reasoning deeply and rigorously; clearly discerning truth from falsehood, the primary from the secondary, and the threads of logic; and putting what you have learned into real action, firmly and concretely.

Together these five stages form a complete loop of cognition and practice in traditional Chinese philosophy. They stress that acquiring knowledge is never simple information reception — it must pass through an internalizing process of critical questioning, independent thought, rational analysis, and embodied practice.

**24. The Feynman learning technique**

The learning philosophy of Richard Feynman, winner of the Nobel Prize in Physics.

The "Feynman technique" is an efficient learning mechanism that uses "active output" to force "deep internalization." It has four core steps — choose a concept; teach it to a layperson (or a child) in the simplest possible language, free of jargon; identify the gaps in your knowledge where your explanation stumbles; go back to the source material and simplify your explanation. The method stresses that true mastery lies not in memorizing technical terms but in whether you can reconstruct complex knowledge into concise, intuitive language and pass it on.

**26. LLM Wiki**

A "large language model plus private knowledge base" paradigm, popularized by Andrej Karpathy and trending in personal knowledge management (PKM) and AI. The architecture combines traditional networked note systems (Markdown-based knowledge bases like Obsidian and Notion) with LLMs, retrieval-augmented generation (RAG), and knowledge-graph technology. Its typical workflow is — input raw material (Raw Data), AI automatically parses, distills, and compiles it (AI Ingest), and it produces structured wiki pages and entity links automatically. The aim of this architecture is a private knowledge space in which both human and AI can interact efficiently.

**29. "Record of the Hall of Treasured Paintings" (Baohui Tang Ji)**

From the famous prose piece "Record of the Hall of Treasured Paintings" (《宝绘堂记》), written by the Northern Song literary figure Su Shi for the imperial son-in-law Wang Shen (courtesy name Jinqing), who was obsessed with collecting rare paintings and calligraphy. The essay invokes the Laozi's line "the five colors blind the eye, the five tones deafen the ear, the five flavors dull the palate," warning that obsessing over external things easily costs a person their inner clarity. In it Su Shi incisively sets out the dialectical relationship between "lodging meaning in things" and "fixing attention on things" —

**Lodging meaning in things** — "A gentleman may lodge his meaning in things, but he may not fix his attention on things." It means entrusting your feelings to external things, using things to carry your aesthetic and intellectual pursuits, playing with things without losing your purpose — not delighted at gaining them, not distressed at losing them.

**Fixing attention on things** — the mind is entirely seized, entangled, and fixated by external things, losing independent subjectivity and sinking into slavery to things ("the gentleman is easily driven by things").
This idea articulates a classical philosophy of the relationship between humans and external tools and objects — to entrust and master them actively, rather than be enslaved by them.
